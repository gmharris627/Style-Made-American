Brand Profile

Red Land Cotton

Worth it?

Yes, if you want bedding that actually comes from somewhere. Red Land Cotton is one of the few home textile brands where the people selling you sheets also grew the cotton that went into them, on land their family has farmed for five generations. The price is real money, but so is the supply chain. Buy during a sale and you're getting genuine farm-to-home American goods at a defensible price.

Commitment

Red Land Cotton's supply chain is about as short as a domestic textile brand can claim. The cotton is grown on the Yeager family's own farm in Moulton, Alabama, not sourced from commodity markets. After harvest and ginning, it goes to spinning and weaving mills in North Carolina and South Carolina, where it's turned into yarn, fabric, and finished goods. Nothing leaves the United States at any stage. The company is transparent that it uses contract mill partners rather than owning its own factories, but the cotton is always theirs, a meaningful distinction in an industry where "American-made" often means only the last sewing step happens domestically.

Character

Red Land Cotton started with a video. In 2015, footage of freshly ginned cotton from the Yeager farm made its way to a family member, who had a simple response: she wanted sheets made from that cotton. Mark Yeager and his daughter Anna, who had worked in New York advertising after studying graphic design at Auburn, decided to find out if that was actually possible. They sourced a set of 1920s-era sheets, had a strip analyzed by Cotton Inc. for reverse engineering, and by October 2016 had their first percale sets ready to sell. The brand takes its name from the distinctive red iron-rich soil the cotton grows in. What makes this model distinctive isn't just the story. It's the structure: a farm family that controls the raw material, skips commodity pricing, and sells directly to the consumer, with their name on both the field and the finished product.

Ethics

In April 2024, the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission recalled approximately 17,400 Red Land Cotton quilts and quilted shams sold between May 2023 and February 2024. The hazard was broken sewing needles found inside quilted products, three consumers reported being jabbed, one with a needle breaking the skin. Red Land Cotton offered replacements or refunds via store credit or check. This appears to be a manufacturing quality-control failure, not a systemic design problem, but it's the most significant product safety issue the brand has faced and shouldn't be minimized. On the labor side, Red Land Cotton states that farm and mill employees earn at or above minimum wage, though the company provides limited public transparency on labor standards beyond that. The Better Business Bureau reflects a handful of unresolved complaints around rewards points and order communication, customer-service gaps more than ethical red flags, but worth noting for a brand that trades on trust. We've reached out to Red Land Cotton for more detail on their labor practices and will update this section when we hear back.

Heritage

The Yeager family has been farming the same Lawrence County, Alabama land for five generations. Mark Yeager was growing cotton there long before the brand existed. The 2016 launch wasn't a pivot into agriculture; it was agriculture deciding to go the rest of the way. That continuity of purpose matters: the people making decisions about Red Land Cotton are the same people looking at the same soil their great-great-grandparents worked. The brand hasn't chased trend cycles or expanded into unrelated categories. It's stayed close to what the land produces and what a household genuinely needs from it.

Value

Queen sheet sets run roughly $175–$260 depending on style; bath towels are $30–$50 each; quilts range from around $200 to over $310 for king sizes. These are premium prices, and they're asking you to pay for the full farm-to-home supply chain rather than just a label. Whether that math works depends on how much the provenance matters to you, but the percale weave does soften with washing rather than degrade, which is the practical sign of well-grown long-staple cotton. The brand runs meaningful sales at Memorial Day, Labor Day, and Black Friday (15–25% off sitewide), and periodic "seconds" sales at 35–45% off for items with minor cosmetic imperfections. Functionally, seconds are identical to first-quality goods, if you're budget-conscious, those sales are the right time to buy.
