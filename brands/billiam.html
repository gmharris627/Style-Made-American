Worth it?

Yes — if you are the right buyer. Billiam makes custom-fit selvedge denim jeans by hand in their Greenville, SC workshop, in American fabric, with 20% of proceeds going to anti-trafficking organizations. At $250–$350 a pair you are paying a fair price for something genuinely rare: jeans built to your body, not a size chart.

Commitment

Billiam is about as all-in as a denim brand can get. Every pair is cut and sewn in-house at their Greenville workshop — no contract factory, no outsourced operations, no overseas component of any kind. Fabric has historically come from Cone Mills' White Oak plant in Greensboro, North Carolina, the benchmark for American selvedge denim. When Cone shuttered White Oak at the end of 2017, Billiam absorbed the disruption by stockpiling remaining White Oak stock and pivoting to other American-milled Cone fabrics, including a line made from recycled materials. The supply chain is a straight line: American mill to South Carolina workshop to customer — with nothing in between.

Character

Bill Mitchell started Billiam in 2009 as a senior at Clemson, after watching a friend tailor his own clothes and deciding to try it himself. He bought a used sewing machine from Goodwill, taught himself to sew a pair of jeans, and within a year had a 400-person waitlist. He spent the next two years building out his skills and equipment from a room in the back of a rock climbing gym. Billiam is not a brand that scaled into a factory — it is a workshop with a storefront. That origin story is not packaging; it is still the operating model. The name is a compression of his first name, and the operation is still his. In a market full of brands that outsource the moment demand spikes, that restraint is its own kind of character.

Ethics

Billiam's fully in-house production model eliminates the opacity that plagues most apparel brands. Workers are employed directly at the Greenville shop — there is no sub-contractor layer to hide behind. The brand's commitment to donating 20% of proceeds to organizations fighting human sex trafficking has been in place since the early days and is treated as a founding principle rather than a marketing line. That is a meaningful number, sustained over years, which tells you something real about the culture of the operation. We've reached out to Billiam for more detail on their labor practices and will update this section when we hear back.

Heritage

Billiam has not drifted. The brand that Bill Mitchell built from a Goodwill sewing machine in 2009 is recognizably the same operation running today: one workshop, one city, one product category, the same fabric sourcing philosophy. The loss of Cone Mills' White Oak plant was a genuine disruption for any brand built around that fabric, and Billiam handled it by adapting without abandoning the domestic-first principle. Seventeen years in, the core proposition — custom-fit American selvedge denim, made by hand in South Carolina — has not been watered down or expanded into a lifestyle brand. That consistency is increasingly rare.

Value

Custom pairs run $250–$350 depending on fabric and cut — slim, straight, or carpenter, for men and women in sizes 28–40. That sits above mass-market selvedge labels like Gustin (which run $100–$175 for off-the-shelf fits) but well below Japanese premium brands like Oni or Samurai, which can exceed $500 for standard cuts. The key difference is what you are buying: Billiam's price includes a made-to-measure fit, in American fabric, sewn by the people whose name is on the label. If you have spent $200 on a pair of raw denim that still does not fit right, the math here is straightforward. For buyers who just want a decent pair of jeans without a consultation process, there are easier options at lower prices — but they are not the same thing.
